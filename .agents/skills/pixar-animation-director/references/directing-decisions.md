# Directing decisions before prompts

This is a practical synthesis of public Pixar teaching and general directing craft, adapted to AI production. It is not a proprietary studio checklist. Sources and attribution boundaries are in `directing-research.md`. Preserve the user's script, generation order, Markdown prompt authority, and no-background-music requirement.

## Scene intention and audience attention

Before choosing shots, state the source scene/beat, whose experience guides the sequence, what the audience knows on entry, and what it should understand or feel on exit. A held emotional state is a valid intention; do not invent a reversal. Identify any scripted reveal and the earliest moment it may be shown or heard. Background objects, reflections, reverse angles, and premature sound cues can accidentally spoil it.

Choose one primary point of attention at a time: a face, a hand, an object, or a relationship in space. Use composition, value contrast, focus, motion, and sound to guide attention in the required order. Avoid giving background activity the same emphasis as the main performance.

## Character performance

For a meaningful beat, resolve stimulus → thought/interpretation → visible response → held result. Thought is a director note; translate it into observable behavior in the executable prompt. Specify what the character is trying to do to another character, not just an emotion label. Treat hidden intention or subtext as a provisional interpretation when the script leaves it open.

Give each character a small, script-compatible movement vocabulary: habitual posture, tempo, use of space, gesture size, and recovery into stillness. Record expression range and bodily constraints in the character bible. Do not give every character identical raised eyebrows, smiles, head tilts, or blinking. Avoid adding tears, lip trembles, or comedy business without script support.

Direct the listener: what receives their attention, what changes on hearing the line, and when the reaction becomes visible. They may hold still. Do not make them anticipate information they have not received. Specify eye targets with geography, not just “expressive eyes.” Use a dominant pose with one readable change; do not independently animate every facial feature.

Character reference sheets stay neutral (face, front, back); do not generate expression sheets unless the user asks. If requested, cover the scene's actual needs, including a neutral/rest state and restrained variants where relevant. Preserve identity through expressions, especially eye spacing, jaw, silhouette, and asymmetry. For unusual anatomy, use available joints and facial mechanisms instead of adding human ones.

## Camera, blocking, and editing

Write a short reason for each important framing choice, camera move, or cut outside the generation prompt. Translate the reason into camera behavior inside it. Use a wide view when the audience needs geography or body relationships; a close view when a small reaction carries the beat. These are choices, not a mandatory wide-to-close sequence.

Choose camera height relative to the character, screen side, viewing angle, camera-to-subject distance, and the needed perspective/focus. Use lens numbers only when useful and supported; numerical specificity alone does not establish intention or guarantee optics. Do not use shallow depth of field when both characters' responses must remain legible.

Plan positions, facing directions, entrances/exits, movement paths, eyeline targets, prop contacts, and the side of the action axis. Keep these consistent across views. A crossing should be visible and motivated when needed. Establish or re-establish geography where the sequence needs it, while protecting scripted concealment.

Cut for new information, a reaction, a changed relationship, or a purposeful rhythm. Hold on a listener when their reception matters more than the speaker. Preserve anticipation, contact, and aftermath long enough to read; do not cut away so early that the cause of the next action disappears. Allow a held shot to carry a complete beat when effective.

## Design, light, and physical credibility

Keep one shared shape/material language across characters, locations, and props. Use clear silhouettes, character-to-prop scale, and uncluttered contact areas. A character sheet should support the required body action, not only look appealing in a neutral pose.

Create a concise progression of palette, brightness contrast, light direction/softness, and focal emphasis across source scenes. Changes should follow the script's time, place, and emotional intent. Keep a consistent base lighting plan within a scene; adjust a shot only as needed for readability without arbitrary light flips. A color-script table is sufficient unless images are requested.

Show believable support and effort: feet support weight, a hand grips the right surface, an object has a visible holder, and motion has a readable start and stop. Match exaggeration to the character and beat. Prioritize staging fixes for contact problems before adding more prompt language.

## Voice, timing, and sound effects

Track separately: voice identity sample, exact script line, performance direction, actual dialogue take, and measured duration. A reference voice sample does not establish the timing or emotion of a new line. Preserve wording, speaker, language, and dialect specified by the user. Mark unavailable listening/transcription capability honestly.

Direct delivery through intention, pace, emphasis, breath, and pauses. Let listening and recovery occupy time. When timing overruns, extend or split the sequence, adjust holds, or use permitted editorial coverage; do not cut or paraphrase lines. Keep overlapping speech only where the script supports it.

Give sound cues a source, onset/action trigger, duration or tail, and perspective. Use synchronized contact sounds and coherent room tone, with space for dialogue. Silence is a deliberate option; avoid filling every pause or decorating every motion with effects. Never add background music. Do not introduce an off-screen sound that implies a new story event.

## Review in useful passes

Apply these to the actual available material. Mark a motion, audio, or timing check as pending when only stills or text exist.

1. **Source fidelity:** every scripted beat and line is covered; no invented action, changed speaker, or early reveal.
2. **Audience comprehension:** the focal subject, geography, cause, and result are readable. View rough boards at thumbnail size and without annotations. Do not claim an audience understood something without audience feedback.
3. **Performance:** the character responds to an identifiable stimulus, listening reads, expressions stay in character, and held poses have purpose. Remove competing gestures before adding intensity.
4. **Timing and cuts:** dialogue fits, reactions have space, action/eyelines match, and the whole sequence has a readable rhythm. Check neighboring shots after any repair.
5. **Image and sound integrity:** identity, scale, anatomy, prop contact, lighting, voice binding, sound synchronization, and absence of background music.

Record the failing shot/time, observable problem, audience consequence, smallest repair, and verification status. Report unsupported story fixes separately. Inspect the first three storyboards for these issues before scaling the established look to the rest; respect the existing user-feedback stage without adding new approval gates.
